Training a large model and hitting 80%+ wall-time overhead from checkpointing sounds like a pathological case. It isn't. At scale, synchronous I/O serializes your training loop against your storage system, and storage almost always loses. Your $600/hour GPU cluster sits idle while a CPU thread moves bytes to disk.

## Why sync checkpointing dominates wall time

The default checkpoint path is three sequential operations:

1. GPU → CPU: transfer model state off the accelerator
2. CPU → Disk: serialize and write to storage
3. Training loop: wait for both to finish before continuing

Step 1 is fast — modern NVLink and PCIe bandwidth can move a checkpoint's worth of state in a few seconds. Step 2 is not. At scale, writing a full model state to network storage can take 30–40 minutes. Until both finish, your GPUs are idle.

For infrequent checkpointing — say, every few hundred steps — you might accept this as a one-time cost. But the moment you want fault tolerance at high frequency, or you're running a long job where checkpoints happen regularly, the overhead compounds. One 40-minute stall every N steps is a fixed tax on every training run.

## The async split

Async checkpointing separates the two steps and only blocks training on the fast one.

**Step 1 (sync, ~1 second):** GPU → CPU transfer. Training pauses briefly while model state moves into CPU memory. This stays synchronous because you need a consistent snapshot — you can't copy model weights while a gradient update is in-flight.

**Step 2 (async, 30–40 minutes):** CPU → Disk upload. A background thread handles the I/O. Training resumes immediately after step 1 finishes. The disk write happens concurrently with the next N steps of training.

Perceived overhead drops from 40 minutes to under a second — a 10×–23× improvement on the checkpoint stall alone. The I/O work is still happening; you just stopped waiting for it.

Ray Train handles this pattern natively. The framework manages the background upload thread and exposes the interface through `report()`.

## What this unlocks

Frequent checkpointing becomes free in the perceived-overhead sense. When the sync cost is ~1 second, there's no reason to checkpoint every 500 steps instead of every 100, or every 100 instead of every 10. You can checkpoint every step if storage bandwidth supports it.

That changes fault recovery. A job that checkpoints every step loses at most one step on failure. For expensive pre-training runs, the difference between recovering from step 10,000 and step 8,500 is real money. You also get the ability to roll back on divergence rather than restarting from a distant save.

## When async still bites you

Three constraints to keep in mind before relying on this pattern.

**CPU RAM headroom.** The checkpoint has to fit in CPU memory during the async upload window. If your model state is large and you're running tight on host RAM, the copy will fail or force you back to synchronous behavior. Size your CPU memory to hold at least one full checkpoint.

**Storage I/O bandwidth.** The background upload needs to finish before the next checkpoint triggers. If your checkpointing interval is shorter than your upload time, you'll stall — the disk write still has to complete eventually. The constraint is that you need `checkpoint_interval × step_time > upload_duration`. At 30–40 minute upload times, very aggressive intervals can still cause pressure.

**`report()` backpressure.** In Ray Train, each `report()` call blocks until the previous background upload finishes. This is intentional — it prevents memory from filling up with queued checkpoints if uploads fall behind. If you call `report()` faster than your storage can drain, you will feel it. This is a safety valve, not a bug, but it's the mechanism worth understanding if you see unexpected stalls.

---

Measure your checkpoint overhead before assuming it's acceptable. If it's over 10 seconds, you're paying for I/O wait in GPU-hours. The fix is straightforward — async checkpointing in Ray Train is a configuration change, not a rewrite. The CPU RAM and storage bandwidth constraints are real but plannable. What's not plannable is a 40-minute stall showing up as a surprise in your training timeline.
